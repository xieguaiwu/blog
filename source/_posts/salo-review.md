Verdict: Among the master's top three works in his entire career. The refined, objective naturalistic audiovisual language successfully commands de Sade's material, and the cultural critique embedded in the 1960s–70s milieu carries considerable value.

On Cinematography: The entire film employs static shots throughout, predominantly medium shots and close-ups, with compositions that frequently feature symmetrical structures yet never feel forced. Most praiseworthy is the exploration of space itself (more on this shortly) — the mirror shots are strokes of brilliance, exploring the very nature of space without any embellishment. One scene in particular is unforgettable, ingeniously using shifts in space to reflect a psychological state, delivering to the viewer the shock of "hearing thunder in utter silence": at the end of the film's third circle, as the four rulers take turns alternating between torturer and spectator, the female pianist — the last vestige of conscience — ascends the dark staircase. Clad in grey, her vivid red hair now swallowed by the abyss, her eyes gently closed, shadow coiled across expressionless features. It is a symmetrical shot. The second-floor corridor behind her leads to the dormitory the girls once occupied; now they are outside in the slaughter-yard, at the mercy of their tormentors. One hundred and twenty days: stripped of clothing, resting here without dignity like beasts, their momentary sleep preparing them only for the next round of inhuman torture that inevitably awaits. Is her hand in this too? She is a pianist of consummate skill, endowed with nothing but talent, yet constrained to convention, playing the lifeless scores unchanged for a century; she is a pianist of consummate skill, endowed with nothing but talent, yet forced to accompany the debauched tales of the madams, watching youth after youth perish before her eyes. She crosses the room; for an instant her body is framed by the window, then she steps without hesitation into the next room, where the wall beside her abruptly shifts, and splashes of colour — vivid, scattered in all directions — reflect the utter ruin of the will to live. No words are needed: she opens the window and leaps.

Continued — On Space: The vast majority of the film takes place in an ancient, oppressive mansion in northern Italy. Pasolini deliberately made it look "like the confiscated home of a wealthy Jew." In Pasolini's hands, certain architectural features of the building are worth noting: on one hand, its structure and décor display a conservative classicism; on the other, the walls of many rooms are covered with avant-garde Futurist paintings. In my view, this contradictory unity symbolizes Italian fascism itself — that incongruous mongrel that blended the authoritarianism of ancient Rome, Italian nationalism that emerged during World War I, radical national syndicalism, and other ideologies, drew fervent support from Italian Futurist artists who championed a distorted Nietzscheanism, and simultaneously allied itself with the old feudal bourgeoisie, using dictatorial terror to suppress the proletariat. One could say that in the film, the oppressors of the vulnerable are not merely the four figures representing the ruling class; this airtight cage that traps everyone within — this grand prison called "the system" — is itself a reactionary force. To render this oppression more thoroughly comprehensible, once the hundred and twenty days of torture begin, the camera almost never turns outdoors. One scene stands out: the entrance of the second "storyteller." The old woman is applying makeup before a mirror when suddenly the roar of fighter planes passes overhead; she draws the curtain to look outside. Pasolini provides no concrete shot — his purpose being the complete separation of the war-torn world beyond.

Continued — On Sound Design: Over nearly two hours, the film uses non-diegetic score only during the opening credits and the ending (the pianist's accompaniment is live recording, which does not count); the most frequently used sound is that of fighter planes. This serves two purposes: first, to heighten the atmosphere when the narrative grows tense; second, to constantly remind the characters (and the audience) of the war raging outside.

On Cultural Critique: Nothing more need be said — this can be found in most reviews.

On Narrative Structure and the Screenplay's Refinement of the Marquis de Sade's Original: What de Sade's original provides is a highly symbolic, allegorical abstract narrative. His erotica contain an obsessively numerical structure (partially quoted below from the article "*The 120 Days of Sodom*: The Writing of Desire and the Subversion of Value"): the book is divided into four chapters, depicting four types of abuse across four winter months. The narrative arranges four masters, four daughters, four storytelling madams. The four libertines represent four temperaments, which in the Greek tradition correspond to the four elements (air, fire, water, earth), the four seasons (spring, summer, autumn, winter), and the four directions (east, south, north, west). Beyond this, all sexual objects are multiples of four: eight girls, eight boys, eight adult concubines... His narrative style can moreover be summarized as a meticulous naturalism influenced by classical works such as *One Thousand and One Nights* and *The Decameron*. Seen in this light, it is understandable that Pasolini, stepping away from his *Trilogy of Life*, chose this particular Sade novel as his source material — and he drastically streamlined the original, adopting instead a *Divine Comedy*-esque progressive structure to paint this picture of hell on earth. What was not mentioned above is that the film's third circle extends the torture from indoors to outdoors, yet the camera's angle remains that of an interior gaze. Even as the young men and women with whom we had sympathized are dragged to the torture ground and subjected to wanton torment, the audience remains trapped within the mansion, unable to move. Pasolini appropriated Sade's elaborate classical narrative, shifting time and space from the end of Louis XIV's reign to the final days of the Republic of Salò. His technique remains continuous with the mature period of the *Trilogy of Life*, only now the content is bolder and the critique more radical.

On Sade, Nietzsche, and Fascism: In this novel by Sade, just when the reader has finally endured the long introduction and is ready to "let loose," there suddenly bursts forth philosophical speculation on freedom, crime, and desire. This not only tells us of the impossibility of deriving pleasure from Sade's books, but also convinces us that he is by no means a mediocrity pandering to vulgar readers. A brief summary of Sade's thought: a liberalism of natural supremacy influenced by the Enlightenment, which asserts that people should reclaim dominion over their own bodies, transgress ethics and traditional binary notions of good and evil — from this perspective, it can even be regarded as a precursor to Nietzscheanism. Some, drawing on Sade's aristocratic origins and the sadistic element in his character, emphasize the negative dimensions of his thought, using this as an opportunity to erroneously bind him together with fascism. But I personally do not agree with this view. First, the vast majority of Sade's works were written during nearly thirty years of imprisonment; precisely for this reason he never suffered physical liquidation during the French Revolution, so his observations from that period can be regarded as relatively objective and de-classed (though I do not reject judging from this angle). To a large extent, he was equally an opponent and profaner of the old order. Second, linking Nietzschean doctrine with fascism — or even with Nazism — has always been unjust, which can be argued in the following ways: (1) Nietzsche's thought is vast and wide-ranging, and fundamentally not a theory to be applied to political systems. Later existentialism also demonstrated Nietzsche's enormous power in relation to individual life. (2) It is true that Nietzsche's works contain "nationalist" rhetoric, but one cannot on this account ignore his era — for instance, in 1871 when he wrote *The Birth of Tragedy*, the German Empire as a nation-state had just been established, and his idealizing of the nascent state through the image of ancient Greece is beyond reproach. Moreover, he simply cannot be lumped together with the fervent nationalists of the early twentieth century (or the Nazis, with their baffling racial theories). *Thus Spoke Zarathustra*, his summation of his thought in later life, expresses precisely his deep aversion to the state apparatus. (3) The book *The Will to Power*, later trumpeted as gospel, was tampered with by Nietzsche's sister, its core doctrine of the will to power distorted into collective external aggression. The statism to which fascism and Nazism amounted itself crushed the possibility of individual sublimation — they are the very antithesis of Nietzscheanism. The four rulers in *The 120 Days of Sodom* engage in dialogue touching on Nietzsche and Baudelaire; they have used the Dionysian spirit as a fig leaf: "Only fascism is truly anarchist" — this is utterly absurd. Their unbridled conduct, from first to last, derives from inherited power and wealth; their debauchery is nothing but the Dionysian spirit — it is merely the repetition, spanning thousands upon thousands of years, of the oppression and exploitation through which desire has been indulged. Enough has been said; it is time to discuss the film's ending: when the four rulers all leave the mansion to carry out their atrocities in the slaughter-yard, when people are dead or maimed, and the rulers too await only the end of the war and the coming of desperate judgment, two Fascist guards, themselves enslaved, turn off the *Carmina Burana* playing on the radio and put on a languid dance tune. Under the serene gaze of the abstract Futurist paintings covering the walls, they join arms and dance, asking each other the names of their girlfriends. They will undoubtedly die, and with them history will consign the crimes of these hundred and twenty days to oblivion — but when slaves perish, their masters are doomed as well...

Note: This article is intended to help other readers understand Michel Foucault's *Death and the Labyrinth: The World of Raymond Roussel* by mapping the book's internal logic (with the exception of Chapter One) and supplementing its external background of composition, as well as offering — as a bilingual reader in Chinese and English who does not know French (and I suspect most readers are in the same position) — several suggestions for improving the Chinese translation.

Foucault's analysis of Roussel begins with a chance encounter in the José Corti bookshop. The writing technique in *La Vue* (*The View*), which was later imitated by Alain Robbe-Grillet, became the starting point of Foucault's personal interest in Roussel, while *How I Wrote Certain of My Books* and its emphasis on deliberate artifice served as the fuse that ignited the philosophical lens's war against Roussel. **"The visible and the invisible," like the loose thread of a sweater, became a major handle by which to cross Roussel's threshold. Alongside it came the displacement of "difference and identity," plus a saturated language-thing space** (or "the space of non-existence beneath the surface of things," or lens-like vastness — Foucault frequently repeats the same meaning in varied forms throughout this book) as the field of its activity — **Foucault uses these two phenomena to locate, within Roussel's own work, the interstices by which it is structured** (just as Roussel, with his playful "process," located necessary death within the contingency of language — the death of language itself and of everything it carried within classical literature), thus commencing the writing of this book.

The first book's chapter describes the process I have just mentioned. It will be tirelessly repeated by Foucault with a profusion of neologisms across the following seven chapters. Indeed, had Foucault been willing to steel himself and delete the repetitive redundancies born of his own complacency in writing *Raymond Roussel* (in an interview with Charles Ruas, the American critic born in Tianjin, he said he completed the book in only two months), the volume could have been reduced by at least one-sixth or even one-fifth, and its difficulty of reading somewhat lowered. But since he himself described his chance encounter with Roussel as "a love affair that lasted several summers," we can be certain that he wrote this unnecessary material not out of vanity but out of unceasing passion. It certainly makes the reading arduous, but it also grants us the immense pleasure that Foucault must have experienced in conceiving his method of analyzing Roussel. The second and third chapters penetrate into the interior of Roussel's post-"Great Youth Period" works, patiently explaining, with a linguistically precise attitude and in constant reference to *How I Wrote Certain of My Books*, the formal origins of the four major representative works that followed. These two chapters are arguably the most difficult in the book, and thus the most passionate as games of intellect and endurance — yet they do not represent the true face or the totality of Roussel, but only the totality of Foucault's personal interpretation of Roussel. The fourth chapter is, among Foucault's discussions of the interior of Roussel's work (the analysis of individual texts and the analysis of the evolution of his creative process), the most important chapter of the book. Its first half concerns the four masterpieces left by Roussel before his death — synthesizing the "works with process" and the "works that do not (similarly) display process" represented by *La Doublure*, *La Vue*, *Le Concert*, and *La Source*. The second half finally draws to a close the analysis conducted from within Roussel's creative career, beginning with its very commencement — the long poem *Mon Âme* (later renamed *L'Âme de Victor Hugo*), published at age seventeen. Incidentally, a foreshadowing planted in the first half of Chapter Four — "They proclaim that she has been saved, telling her, 'The Emperor was most impressed and expresses complete satisfaction, for this young woman has fulfilled all the conditions strictly set by the Emperor.' But in the kingdom where Roussel lived in solitude, there was no Emperor, no admiration, and no bestowed grace" — becomes, in Chapter Eight, the wedge with which Foucault introduces the psychologist Pierre Janet's diagnosis of Roussel: "And this language surrounding Roussel, which once shone from the depths of its smallest syllables like enchanted water, now dissipated in a world without spectators: 'When this young man walked into the street with intense emotion, he found that people did not turn to look at him as he passed, and the sense of glory and radiance suddenly went dark.'"

The fifth chapter is the turning point of the book. It introduces the two concepts of "metamorphosis" and "labyrinth" to replace the language-focused interpretation of the second and third chapters. In brief, the meaning of metamorphosis here encompasses both the linguistic and the plot levels, for although in fact "being and language share no common system... it is language alone that constitutes the system of being," yet *Seul en France* (*Solitary in France*), influenced by Jules Verne, and the crucial *Nouvelles Impressions d'Afrique* (*New Impressions of Africa*) — compared to texts like *La Vue*, in which "language resumes the ordinary route of enumeration (of beings)" — display a plot and plot-internal objects that appear far more substantial: "**All these devices open a closed space that is at once protective and magically connective**." This metamorphosis, which contains a fundamental problem in Roussel's creative work, is a metamorphosis with an origin. It manifests through the arduous performance of paired existents within the plot ("In Roussel's positive world, the patience of the animal-tamer replaces the power of the magician") — or rather, the sum of such performances, that is, the theater. Through the joint tracing of language and plot, we can finally see the thing beneath the theater: the labyrinth, which is "at once the truth and the nature of the Minotaur, that which encloses it from without and that which clarifies it from within." Yet the convergence of identity offered by this labyrinth is not the Minotaur, but a mirror — a mirror of death and birth, the deep and inaccessible chamber of all metamorphosis. The question of "birth" is discussed in greater detail in Chapter Eight.

From this point onward, Foucault analyzes Roussel from a further distance, and this method in turn causes us, by reflection, to perceive more acutely the function of the "lens" in *La Vue*. Is not the very analytical terminology Foucault has chosen — "outside of Marxism or phenomenology" — not identical to this pen that carries the lens? ("The pen in *La Vue* — it is this pen, and not anything else whatsoever, that wrote the works with process; for this pen itself is the process, or more precisely, the incomprehensible intimation of the process: a machine embedded in the linguistic instrument that makes things seen.") Without the more distant analysis of the fifth chapter, we would not perceive the foreshadowing Foucault planted in his brief analysis of *La Doublure* in Chapter Two, the other layer of whose value would not be revealed until Chapter Six.

The sixth chapter concerns Roussel's two texts without process: *La Vue* and *La Doublure*. The former is the more important of the two. The opening pages continue the visibility (theater) and invisibility (labyrinth) revealed in the previous chapter, while introducing two further concepts: the relationship between linguistic modality and process, and the exposure and shadow that are constantly dragged and repeated — "this invisibility rooted in exposure itself is nothing other than the pure and simple visibility that exposure brings to light in its indifference." The former recedes to a secondary position in the seventh chapter's analysis of *Nouvelles Impressions d'Afrique*, while the latter becomes one of the primary subjects of the seventh chapter.

Foucault first addresses the relationship between Roussel's verse works and his prose works — the latter described by Robbe-Grillet as "a transition from an idiotic murmur to a harsh, industrious clamour." The technique of repeated words embedded in *La Vue* occupied the dominant position only during the brief period in which Roussel abandoned verse. Thus it is a substitute for the verse process, still concealing a secret within it ("Sometimes this language leaves discourse in a state without process; at other times, it is discourse with process. But the former is written in verse, the latter in prose."). Its connection to *La Doublure*, another text without process, greatly elevates its status within Roussel studies (the last sentence is my own summary).

Foucault then begins his analysis of *La Vue*, but before summarizing his analysis, I wish to outline the contours of this work by inserting a passage by Robbe-Grillet: "...everything appears to be in complete motion, yet has already congealed within this motion, immobile within representation, interrupting all action, all falling, all rolling, bringing them to eternity in the instant before their ultimate moment, severing them forever from meaning." We can see that although Foucault's discussion of Roussel's visibility still derives from the concept of "theater" in Chapter Five, what he truly wishes to do is use the differences within the identity of the concepts of "theater" and "view" to push the former aside and clarify the latter. *La Vue* is a work possessing a visibility that does not display itself: "It is like a gift offered by a deep opening in the things themselves. No sharp instrument is needed to penetrate things and make them tell their secrets." Yet "all that is visible is never wholly seen; it always gives something else that still remains to be seen; one never reaches the end." "All language and all seeing emerge from this marvellous and secret interweaving (that is, the interweaving of word-image, or of language and thing — its root being the interweaving of the lacking signifier and signified). The process hidden beneath the narrative of *Nouvelles Impressions d'Afrique* and *Seul en France* is precisely this interweaving; what *How I Wrote Certain of My Books* reveals is precisely this interweaving." It is precisely for this reason that Foucault requires the more distant analysis of Chapter Five to dissect this illusion of identity.

Beyond this argument that Foucault repeats throughout the book, another key point of this chapter concerns the metaphor of "the visible and the invisible": "Being defines itself in an extremely rich unbiasedness; light traverses being thoroughly, inexhaustibly. This light, outside of time, is an eternal and gentle emanation"; "Upon the surface of harsh light are the play of instants, the flashes that alight upon the surface of objects — forming a sudden, fleeting, rapidly vanishing contact, biting into a corner or a bulge, yet they make things shimmer — never penetrating them, leaving them intact, stubbornly there, in their prior presence before things"; and "In this linguistic instrument, in this lens with which one looks... we recognize another dawn industry... This is the process beginning from the dawn of a primitive and original state, the process without process, so dazzling as to be invisible. And the other is the noon industry of dawn; one must conceal it well in order to see it. Perhaps it is their separation that casts shadow without shelter." This metaphor of radiance and shadow is further developed in the seventh and eighth chapters.

The source of the radiance is precisely *La Doublure*, mentioned as early as Chapter Two, where it appeared only as a representative of an early embryonic form of the process. In Foucault's discussion of it, we can glimpse something of the fourth chapter: "The mask appears as a true yet false double; language, meticulously covering the face, enumerates its every flaw, slipping within the space that separates it from the person it replicates (that is, its lining)." But in this chapter, the gap manifested in the plot's opening (called, in the quoted passage, "flaws [in the mask's] face") overlaps with the concepts of "theater" and "performance" discussed earlier. If at first we could discern in this section — appearing abruptly after the analysis of *La Vue* — only the prototype of "theater" and the gap of linguistic identity that Foucault has already repeated tirelessly again and again, then the deeper exploration of the mask in the final pages of this chapter causes us to truly understand Foucault's intention: what he wishes to emphasize is not the relationship between *La Vue* and *La Doublure*, but their radically different aspects. *La Vue* presents the surface of things, while *La Doublure* reveals the mask of things — that is, the labyrinth beneath the theater — and the method by which this mask comes into being: "The slight opening of the mouth that causes the mask to collapse is simultaneously the thing that presents the mask fully to sight, that which establishes the mask in its true being. The fissure splits the double and immediately constitutes the double in its marvellous unity... The function of language is to make the visible visible by doubling it (doubler is the process by which existents become doubles; double is the double itself), and thereby to show that in order for the visible to be seen, it needs to be repeated by language; only words root the visible in things." Until *Nouvelles Impressions d'Afrique*, Roussel's works were all "the relationship between language and a non-existent space, a space of non-existence beneath the surface of things, separating the interior of things' visible aspect from the periphery of their invisible core." Thus the second and third chapters, focused on the interior of language, and the fifth chapter, standing at a further distance, gradually diverge — and now, at the conclusion of the sixth chapter, the former is decisively pushed aside.

The seventh chapter is devoted entirely to Roussel's final poem, *Nouvelles Impressions d'Afrique* — a work of extraordinary structural and compositional complexity, which took thirteen years to complete. In *How I Wrote Certain of My Books*, Roussel revealed nothing whatsoever of the mysteries of its composition, in contrast to his boastful discussions of his earlier works: "It is as though Roussel could speak only of the obscurity of this work, only of the very portion of it into which the light he truly wrote dispersed — the dark edge of the work."

The verses of *Nouvelles Impressions d'Afrique* are full of sudden supplements and extensions enclosed in parentheses. If the original sentence is considered the zero degree, then the levels of these parentheses can reach up to the fifth degree. To represent the distinct hierarchical relationships between the parentheses, Roussel uses five superimposed parentheses to indicate the level of nesting. Beyond the multiplicity of superimposed levels, another characteristic of these supplements and extensions is that they branch out laterally within the same level, like bushes: "Within parentheses that have four branches can be juxtaposed (remaining outside each other) five layers wrapped around two buds. Two or three, or even more. Similarly, three can support multiple fourth-level systems, the second degree can support multiple third-level systems, and so on." But the complexity generated merely by producing so many levels within the original text would not have been sufficient to occupy this immensely diligent writer — who, on account of his obsession, would on average spend fifteen hours a day polishing his rhymes — for thirteen long years. Another major characteristic of *Nouvelles Impressions d'Afrique* is the numerous footnotes at the bottom of each page, like the roots of the text. "These footnotes are arranged in the form of twelve-syllable verse, so that provided the reader carefully reads the corresponding footnote at the point marked, they can always find the consistent continuation of the rhyme (if the first line of the footnote's verse does not rhyme with the preceding line, it certainly rhymes with the line of verse just inserted into the text; if the final line of the footnote's verse remains unresolved, it rhymes with the first line of verse where the text resumes)..."

Foucault compares *Nouvelles Impressions d'Afrique* with *La Vue*: the former is "a constructed discourse," "each new circle inscribed within the wood of the verse requires an overall adjustment; only by establishing the center of this circular [annual-ring-like] growth (the most recent and also the most interior) can the system find its balance. Each advance of this inner growth must be completely subversive of the language it expands. The invention of each line of verse is both the destruction of the whole and the prescription for rebuilding the whole." The latter is a work that "approaches the minutiae of things" by "describing a thing."

**Let us end the present essay here — I have no interest in continuing further.**

Areas where the Chinese translation could be improved: Roussel's works should be arranged in chronological order (or in the order of Roussel's creative mechanisms). Foucault's explanatory order is already quite erratic, and such an arrangement would help readers better grasp the book's overall thread.

> 📝 **Article Type**: Personal Essay

There is an empirical observation that is becoming more and more self-elucidating: the openness and closeness of a society need not correlate with social performance, from individual autonomy to the source of authority. Popper's defining variables (tribalism vs. individualism, critical rationalism, the source of authority) are performance-loaded and not observable before the fact. I find that the influential variables can be cut down to a smaller group, including:

- $S$ spatial encloseness
- $T$ time-control intensity
- $F$ flow-control intensity

where $T$ and $F$ together constitute population management.

The Chinese high school is where these three levers are turned to their limit: a miniature of the closed society.

Throughout the years of school in China, what has always been astonishing is the school's grip on the mind, and its inefficiency. The figures are unambiguous: China has, in most high schools, 12-14 actual school hours per day (including evening self-study sessions), yielding a 60-70-hour in-school period per week, whereas the average OECD nation has only 27 in-class hours per week, less than half the Chinese figure. A prevalent myth is that this intensity of study is needed to cultivate a generation of well-trained students, who will be the very pillars of the state. However, the reality is that the Chinese government has just released hundreds of millions of highly degreed, mentally disabled, and myopic youth as the new blood of food delivery and ride-hailing. The inefficiency in human resource utilization is similar to that in the time management of high school. Both are driven by the same logic that the individuals (businessmen and students) do not have the ability, the willpower, or the foresight to decide on their own, thus requiring an omnipresent patriarch-like figure to be in charge. It is true, though, that both the private economy and the majority of high school students would do worse if they are let free and cut loose in their current state, but that is entirely entailed by the overly arbitrary control that has destroyed the self-resilience and spontaneous regulation of both the market and individual students.

During my days back in junior high school, my weight had once peaked at around 90 kilograms (200 lbs), with an extremely high body fat percentage. This is a bitter fruit that I had to bear for years, until I determined to change, and took six months to lose 30 kilograms (70 lbs) of weight purely through exercise and a transformation to a healthier diet. Let me describe my day at that time: at 6:30, I get up just to catch the morning study session, though our class officially begins at 8 o'clock and ends at 5:30. After almost 9 hours staying on my chair, I still have to endure an evening self-study session until 9:30. It is said that the self-study sessions are left for us to manage, yet the flood of homework and other kinds of assignments makes it impossible to keep any time for myself. I was completely exposed in a classroom that holds 45 people, the fat kid who took up the largest space in class; what is destroyed is the self and the peace that can only grow where a person owns his own time and space. Back home at ten, the things that I have sacrificed for, reportedly, a good mark are considered to be fictional. The Chinese culture, the wisdom that Confucius taught, leaves no place for individual autonomy, nor dignity and freedom. To compensate myself, I end my day with burgers, pizzas, cans of soda or other abominable junk food. They do not fill in the hollow in my heart, especially not after inducing vomiting.

Nothing in that day was accidental; each item was one of the three channels doing its work, and the catalogue extends beyond my own timetable. The morning session and evening self-study hold $T$: hours are allocated by decree before any learning is designed, and what gets audited is the hour, not the comprehension. The mandatory lecture and the Monday flag-raising ceremony hold $F$: there is no exit and no feedback, no channel through which the verdict "this was useless" could ever travel. The classroom and the campus wall hold $S$: every hour is lived in a place the student did not choose, under forty-five pairs of eyes that did not ask. What such a system can observe is presence, so presence is what gets produced: the twelfth hour of evening study is worth little as learning and full as evidence of control.

The private economy is governed by the same three levers and answers with the same strategy. Its time ($T$) is claimed by whatever the authority can see (inspection teams, meetings, study sessions, the firm's own flag-raising), while its investment calendar follows the policy cycle rather than demand. Its entries and exits ($F$) are licensed: credit is allocated by political identity rather than profitability (the more unprofitable a firm, the more it borrows, and a state firm's interest burden runs nearly three times a private one's), entire industries are opened and closed by administrative order, and judicial bankruptcy is so rare (some 48,000 cases in fourteen years nationwide) that failure is postponed rather than settled, so capital cannot leave where it is wasted. Its permitted space ($S$) is drawn in advance by business scope, land quotas and entry permits, and inside that fenced field firms compete for the overseer's signals rather than for customers.

Both systems therefore select on compliance instead of product, because compliance is the only thing an overseer can watch. The worker's ledger mirrors the student's: 2,328 hours a year against Germany's 1,335, and per-hour output below the world median: 17.7 international dollars against America's 83.5, only 81st of 129 economies. High input, middling return, the same inversion in both columns. This is why $S$, $F$ and $T$ are the key variables: they are the knobs the authority actually turns, and reforms that touch content while leaving the channels sealed (a new textbook, a "reduce the burden" notice, relief for private firms while credit allocation and exit rules stay untouched) inherit the old equilibrium, because as long as time is allocated from above, exit requires permission and space is walled, performing remains the cheapest strategy and presence remains the metric. Open one channel and the premium on performance collapses: free exit makes waste visible, self-owned time makes presence worthless, open space removes the ability to count it.

If the timetable is the first instrument of closure, the examination is the second, and it works not through the schedule but through the design of the questions.

In the sciences, the syllabus itself is sealed to a narrow band of knowledge points: school physics is kept calculus-free, so students master a mechanics that cannot speak the language the discipline itself speaks, and their picture of reality is sealed off from the reality physicists describe. Difficulty, capped from above, then has to come from somewhere; it migrates into the question. Problems are dressed in elaborate scenarios and studded with sign errors, boundary cases and traps, so that the examination certifies skill at divining the examiner's intention rather than mastery of physics: the student is lured into error instead of being tested on what he actually knows.

The language arts turn the same design against the capacities themselves: it is the one subject whose raw material is the student's own reading, yet personal appreciation is graded against a fixed reading, non-template writing is penalized, and the daily flood of repetitive drills crowds out the autonomous reading that alone could build the capacity the examination claims to measure. The system examines a skill while making its production impossible.

Geography completes the picture: real geography works with data, field evidence, models and uncertainty, while the examination replaces the world with scenarios invented by the question-setter, a fabricated place that answers to no evidence at all, so students learn to reason inside a manufactured reality, disciplined by the examiner rather than by facts.

Three subjects, one design. The examination hall holds the parameters at their maximum: the student is sealed in a room ($S$), the clock rules ($T$), and there is no exit, no question, no appeal ($F$). Inside that closure, the design of the questions corrupts the metric itself: difficulty is manufactured through traps, so selection rewards trap-avoidance and deference to authority. These are precisely the traits a closed society needs and an open market punishes.

So the miniature reproduces the whole: channels sealed, metric corrupted, compliance rewarded, and a performance celebrated on paper. The figures that opened the argument (more than twice the OECD's class hours) were never the indictment by themselves; they are only what the indictment looks like on a calendar. A society that mistakes presence for effort and obedience for ability will get exactly the economy those definitions promise: abundant compliance, scarce productivity, and a hollow where the self used to be.

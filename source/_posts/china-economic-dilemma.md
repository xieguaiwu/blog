## 1. Definitions: The National Economy in Modern Capitalist Systems

Just as any doctrine that aspires to diagnose individual psychological problems must, explicitly or implicitly, presuppose an ideally healthy psychological function, so too must my diagnosis be grounded in such a presupposition. To ensure the rigour of my argumentation, I will not rely solely on the Circular Flow diagram or the specific national models of Keynes and other schools to establish this foundational state. Instead, I choose a more abstract approach to define a foundational structure. The reader will have no difficulty establishing connections between this structure and specific doctrines, but need not confine themselves to the presuppositions of any single school.

## 2. Comparing the Chinese Economic System

The core difference between China and mainstream economies in East Asia and elsewhere lies in its political system, which I will not elaborate upon here — but the reader must remain constantly aware that all economic phenomena discussed in this section and beyond derive from the particularity of this political system.

### 2.1 Health Standards and Chinese Reality by Layer
| Circuit Layer | Health Standard | Chinese Reality | Nature of Deviation |
|---|---|---|---|
| **P1 Production Layer** | Labour is fully employed | The main engine of employment is small and medium-sized private enterprises, yet they are the most difficult sector to finance (see Song & Zhang, 2018)<br/>(Note: Song & Zhang (2018) demonstrate SME financing difficulty and credit risk differentials, not employment share; the latter is a widely recognized fact of the Chinese economy) | Employment structure and financing structure misaligned |
| **P2 Distribution Layer** | Labour remuneration tracks productivity | Average profit margin of SOEs: **-0.5%**; private enterprises: **13.5%**; but SOEs' interest-to-sales ratio is nearly **3×** that of private firms (6.2% vs. 2.2%); credit and profit margin are **negatively correlated** (−0.008\*\*\*) (see Cull et al., 2009)<br/>(Note: The above profit margin and interest data are based on Cull et al.'s (2009) analysis of the Chinese Industrial Enterprises Database for 1998–2003, now over twenty years old; timeliness should be noted) | Distribution decoupled from efficiency |
| **P3 Consumption Layer** | Wages sufficient for labour reproduction | Wage growth suppressed (see P2); transfer payments weak (P6) | Consumption layer contracts |
| **P4 Accumulation Layer** | Savings converted into efficient investment | Monetary expansion stalls upstream: M2 +1% → upstream short-term borrowing **+0.68%** (significant); downstream **-0.12%** (not significant); upstream incremental credit does not convert into trade credit to downstream (see Cun et al., 2022) | Transmission broken |
| **P5 Financial Layer** | Credit flows to where productivity is highest | Credit allocated by political identity; the lower tier relies on shadow banking/P2P — peak 100.4 trillion RMB (123% of GDP), then administratively compressed; P2P cleared to zero within one year (see *China Shadow Banking Report*, 2020; Liu et al., 2024) | Allocation rights politicized |
| **P6 State Layer** | Rules predictable; losers exit | Regulatory "one-size-fits-all" administrative U-turns; judicial bankruptcies extremely rare (see *Supreme People's Court Work Report*, 2021); administrative deregistration substitutes for them | Administration replaces law |

#### 2.1.1 P1 ↔ P5 (Credit Allocated by Political Identity Rather Than Productivity)

The negative correlation between credit and efficiency is the core reason why the P1 production layer has gradually moved toward depression in recent years under the impact of exceptional events. Cull et al. (2009), using panel data of over 100,000 industrial enterprises per year from 1998–2003, found that the average sales profit margin of state-owned enterprises was **-0.5%** (a loss), while that of private enterprises was **13.5%**. Yet the interest burden of SOEs (interest/sales: 6.2%) was nearly three times that of private firms (2.2%). The regression is even more direct: **the lower the enterprise's profit margin, the more bank credit it receives** (coefficient -0.008, significant at 1%).

> What is presented in this subsection is not the whole picture. Interested readers may consult China's specific liquidation mechanisms for "zombie enterprises" and recent data, which can supplement certain dimensions of this economic reality, but are insufficient to constitute a rebuttal of Section 2.1.1.

#### 2.1.2 Monetary Expansion Stalls Upstream

Credit allocation cannot be separated from monetary policy. The findings of Cun et al. (2022) are rather stark: during monetary expansion periods, for every 1% increase in M2 growth, short-term borrowing in upstream industries (the sectors where large enterprises and SOEs are concentrated) increases by 0.68% (statistically significant), while the change in downstream industries is -0.12% (not significant).

More importantly, **the incremental credit obtained by upstream industries does not convert into trade credit to downstream industries**. In theory, once upstream obtains liquidity, it can transmit funds to downstream SMEs through supply chain payment terms (payables/receivables). In practice, this pipeline is virtually blocked. Trade credit carries positive externalities, its supply is inherently insufficient under decentralized decision-making, and this externality intensifies during recessions — meaning **precisely when transmission is most needed, transmission efficiency is at its lowest**.

#### 2.1.3 P2 Layer

Song & Zhang (2017), using 16,817 loans to unlisted SMEs, provide a clear picture: collateralized loans account for **71.7%**, third-party guaranteed loans 14.2%, while pure credit loans constitute only **1.4%** — indicating that relationship-based lending barely exists in Chinese SME lending; banks recognize only hard information and collateral. And the scarcest credit loans? **51.3% flow to state-owned enterprises**.

When private enterprises have in reality become the main engine of urban employment in China, their financing channels remain only a narrow gate of "collateral + guarantees + trade credit payment terms." Narrow gates mean constrained expansion and weak wage bargaining power; thus **the distribution layer (P2) is structurally suppressed by the financial layer (P5)**. The contraction of the consumption layer (P3) is not that "Chinese people don't like to spend" — it is that the wage link has been systematically suppressed. Ultimately, the costs of labour reproduction are shifted onto the household interior.

#### 2.1.4 Shadow Banking's Buffering Role Against the Three Preceding Dilemmas
> *China Shadow Banking Report*: Shadow banking can be divided into broad and narrow senses. Broad shadow banking includes interbank wealth management and other bank wealth management products, bank interbank investments in specific purpose vehicles, entrusted loans, trust loans, non-stock public funds, securities asset management, insurance asset management, asset securitization, non-equity private funds, online P2P lending institutions, loans provided by financial leasing companies and small loan companies, commercial factoring company receivables, financing guarantee company obligations, consumer loans issued by unlicensed institutions, and debt financing plans and structured financing products provided by local exchanges. Among these, interbank specific purpose vehicle investments and interbank wealth management, wealth management investments in non-standard debt claims, entrusted loans, trust loans, online P2P lending, and non-equity private funds exhibit pronounced shadow banking characteristics with relatively high risk, constituting narrow shadow banking.

The official report provides staggering figures: broad shadow banking reached a historical peak of **100.4 trillion RMB** in early 2017, accounting for **123%** of that year's GDP; bank wealth management, trust loans, entrusted loans, and interbank investments were layered in nested structures. By the end of 2016, entrusted loans had swollen to 13.97 trillion RMB.

The report itself acknowledges that the fundamental difference between Chinese shadow banking and that of other economies lies in its **primary purpose of regulatory arbitrage**. Its rise has structural reasons: the central government controls aggregate volumes (credit quotas), local governments seek investment (financing platforms, infrastructure, land development), and the intermediate product of this game is shadow banking. Its decline likewise originated from political decisions: nearly 16 trillion RMB was compressed over three years of rectification from 2017–2019 (interbank wealth management shrank from 6.8 trillion to 0.84 trillion); over 700 million RMB in fines were levied and nearly 10,000 institutions were penalized. Online P2P lending went from a peak of approximately 1 trillion USD, covering bottom-tier borrowers with credit scores of 511, to the entire industry cleared to zero by the end of 2020. Liu et al.'s empirical research shows that the shift from "comprehensive encouragement" to "comprehensive shutdown" took approximately one month, and the borrowers (rural, young, without bank records) in the cleared industries suffered the most.

China is decidedly not a ordinary modern capitalist economic system maintained on the basis of a legal regime; the survival of its bottom-tier financing channels does not depend on market supply and demand but on administrative decisions.

##### 2.1.4.1 Supplement: Policy Counterpositioning and Its Limits

It is true that official attempts at resolution have been made, but they are powerless to reverse the situation. Zhang Shaohui (2025)'s empirical analysis of the 2014 government-bank-guarantee pilot shows that after the tripartite risk-sharing (risk-sharing 15%–30%) by government, banks, and guarantee institutions, new fixed-asset investment by small and micro enterprises grew by **15.34%** (significant at 1%) — meaning government credit can indeed lever open the gates of credit. But the same paper records the cost: after the pilot, banks' **loan loss provisions and risk reserves declined significantly**, because when risk is shared by the government, the incentive for banks' due diligence is weakened — "free-riding" is structural.

Policy counterpositioning here falls into a kind of self-referential trap: it can open the floodgates of credit quantitatively, but it cannot substitute for risk pricing. And the deeper government credit intervenes, the more distorted price signals become.

#### 2.1.5 The Absence of Exit Mechanisms Makes Self-Repair of the Cycle Impossible

In truth, I initially hoped to use systems theory to characterize the formalized description at the paper's opening, and could even follow the approach of Guan Jitong (see *Prosperity and Crisis*). Had I truly followed this path, this section would have pointed directly to a flaw in the Chinese economic system as a non-steady-state system — namely, its inability **to allow failing enterprises to exit normally through effective clearing mechanisms, thereby releasing labour and capital back into the cycle**. In the fourteen years from the implementation of the new Bankruptcy Law in 2007 to 2020, courts nationwide concluded approximately 48,000 bankruptcy cases (an average of just over 3,000 per year), while enterprise exits were primarily completed through commercial deregistration (an administrative procedure). The extreme rarity of judicial bankruptcy means: debts are not liquidated through the courts but are indefinitely postponed through renewed loans, loan extensions, and new loans to repay old ones.

Similar to the zombie enterprises that emerged in Japanese society after the bursting of the bubble, here they manifest primarily as dead capital in the P4 layer: loss-making SOEs kept alive by credit (see Argument 1); zombie enterprises occupying resources and labour, the cycle's self-repair mechanism replaced by political objectives. At this point, **losses are merely erased from the books; they continue to accumulate elsewhere in the form of leverage and bad debt**.

## 3. Summary
Connecting the six arguments, the "normal state" of the Chinese system forms a closed loop:

1. **Politicization of the financial layer** (2.1.1) → Credit misallocation
2. Misallocation suppresses labour remuneration (2.1.3) → **Consumption layer contracts**
3. Insufficient consumption → The cycle relies on investment and exports for maintenance → **Overcapacity, debt accumulation** (2.1.2 & 2.1.4)
4. Debt pressure → Growth slows → Political target pressure → Re-stimulus (looser credit, more infrastructure) → Higher leverage
5. Absence of exit mechanisms (2.1.5) → Problems not exposed, not liquidated → Until leverage constraints approach, policy oscillates between "stable growth" and "risk prevention"
